# McGuffin

A McGuffin is the thing everyone in the story wants. It does not matter what it is — a
briefcase, a stolen file, a princess, a key. What matters is that people will cross a room,
a city or a galaxy to get it, and that everyone in the story agrees it is worth something.

Hitchcock popularised the word. His own explanation was that the object itself is empty: the
audience never needs to know what is inside the briefcase, only that two men are willing to
kill each other over it.

## The test

Swap the object out. If the story still works with something else in its place, you have a
McGuffin.

The One Ring is **not** a McGuffin. Replace it with a shopping list and the story collapses,
because what the Ring *is* — a thing that tempts you to use it, and therefore must be
destroyed rather than carried — is the theme. A McGuffin is a reason to move. The Ring is a
reason to change.

## In a game it does a second job

In film a McGuffin explains why the camera goes somewhere. In a game it explains why the
**player** goes somewhere, which is more work. "Find the thing" is a verb a player can carry
for twenty hours; "understand your father" is not.

This is why the quest marker on your HUD is usually a McGuffin made visible. The design
question is not "what is the object" but "what does chasing it make the player do".

## Where it goes wrong

- **The McGuffin is treated as the theme.** If your ending is "you got the artifact", the
  player has finished a delivery job and the story has no second half.
- **No stakes.** The player never sees what losing looks like, so the object is worth nothing.
  Show one person who wanted it and did not get it.
- **No texture.** "An ancient relic" is the default, and players forget it by the next room.
  One concrete detail — it is warm, it hums, it is somebody's wedding ring — survives.

## Using it honestly

If your story is about the journey, say so and make the McGuffin cheap. *Mario*'s princess is
in another castle, and the game is in on the joke. If your story is about the object, then it
is not a McGuffin: build it like a character, with a want and a cost.

## Check yourself

??? question "A game asks the player to collect 50 shards to open a door. Is the shard a McGuffin?"
    Yes, and it is a lazy one. Fifty identical shards are a *count*, not a goal: no scene
    shows anyone wanting them, and nothing changes when one is picked up. It still functions —
    it gives the player a direction — but it is doing only the mechanical half of the job.

??? question "Why does 'rescue the princess' hold up in a 1985 platformer and collapse in a twenty-hour RPG?"
    In the platformer the journey is the content, so the goal can be a placeholder. In the
    RPG the player spends hours in scenes, and a placeholder goal leaves those scenes without
    anything to be about.

??? question "You are told your villain wants 'a powerful artifact'. What is the cheapest fix that makes it stop being generic?"
    Give it one concrete property and one cost of using it. Concreteness is remembered;
    "powerful" is not.
