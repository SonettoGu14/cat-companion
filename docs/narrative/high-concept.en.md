# High concept

A high concept is a premise you can state in one sentence that a stranger can repeat back to
you — and whose premise is doing the selling. The term comes from Hollywood in the seventies
and eighties, when studios discovered that a film whose idea fits on a billboard is easier to
greenlight than a film whose idea needs a paragraph.

The classic shorthand: *Alien* is "Jaws in space". *Speed* is "a bus that cannot slow down".

## The one-sentence test

Say it out loud. Then check four things:

- Can you say it without "and then"? If the sentence needs a second sentence, it is a
  synopsis, not a concept.
- Does it imply the whole experience — genre, tone, and what you will be doing for ten hours?
- Is there something in it that is not generic? "A hero must save the world" passes a grammar
  check and fails this one.
- Does the listener ask a follow-up question, or say "oh, like that other game"? The first is
  a concept; the second is a comparison.

## In games, the mechanic usually is the concept

Film concepts describe a situation. Game concepts describe a verb.

- *Braid*: a platformer where you can rewind time — and dying is the puzzle, not the failure.
- *Portal*: a puzzle game where you move through walls you make yourself.
- *Return of the Obra Dinn*: an insurance auditor reconstructs the deaths of a ship's crew
  from frozen moments.

None of those sentences mention a plot. Notice that each one tells you what you will *do*,
which is the only thing a player buys.

A sentence that only describes story — "a young woman discovers her destiny" — is a film
pitch. Add the verb and it becomes a game.

## A formula that works for a first draft

**Someone + wants something + against an obstacle + with a twist.**

- Weak: *a courier delivers a package across a broken world.*
- Stronger: *a courier in a flooded city must carry a package she is not allowed to open, and
  every delivery costs her a memory.*

The second one works because the twist changes the verb, not just the scenery.

## Two traps

**"X meets Y" is a thinking tool, not a pitch.** It is useful for finding the idea in the
first place — "a management game meets a horror film" — but said out loud to an audience, it
announces that your idea is two borrowed ideas.

**High concept is not quality.** It is a door-opener. Plenty of high-concept games are dull,
and plenty of low-concept games are great. What a high concept buys you is that someone else
can repeat your idea without you in the room.

## Why it matters even if nobody is pitching

- **It is a scope test.** If you cannot write the sentence, you do not yet know what you are
  building, and the scope will keep growing while you find out.
- **It is a design filter.** Every feature that does not serve the sentence is optional. That
  is how you cut things without regret: the sentence is the ruler.
- **It is a document header.** The first line of a design document is read more carefully than
  anything after it.

## Check yourself

??? question "Why does 'a bus that cannot slow down' work as a concept where 'a story about a bus' does not?"
    Because the constraint is the premise: it tells you the situation, the tone and the verbs
    at once. "A story about a bus" tells you nothing you can build.

??? question "You have one sentence that describes the plot but not what the player does. Which half is missing?"
    The verb. Game concepts are bought by the action, so the sentence has to contain what the
    player will be doing for most of the playtime.

??? question "Is a high concept necessary for a good game?"
    No. It is necessary for a game that has to be *explained*, which includes every coursework
    project that will be read by someone who was not in the room when you had the idea.
