# The Hero's Journey

Joseph Campbell read myths from many cultures and argued they share one shape: someone leaves
the ordinary world, is tested, and comes back changed. Christopher Vogler turned that into
twelve working stages for screenwriters, and those twelve stages have been in every pitch
deck since.

| Stage | What happens | What it usually is in a game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary World | life before the story | title screen, tutorial, the home village |
| Call to Adventure | something demands a response | the inciting quest, the invasion |
| Refusal of the Call | the hero hesitates | *(usually deleted — see below)* |
| Meeting the Mentor | someone hands over knowledge or a tool | tutorial NPC, an item, a tooltip |
| Crossing the Threshold | leaving the known world | the first area past the hub |
| Tests, Allies, Enemies | learning how this world works | the middle of the game |
| Approach | closing in on the source of the trouble | the dungeon before the dungeon |
| The Ordeal | the darkest moment, a loss | the boss, the death, the betrayal |
| The Reward | what was won | a new ability, an item, the truth |
| The Road Back | returning, with consequences | backtracking through a changed world |
| Resurrection | the final test, transformed | the final boss |
| Return with the Elixir | bringing something home | the ending, New Game+, the sequel hook |

## Why copying it literally fails in games

- **Refusal of the call is a film beat.** The player already accepted the adventure by pressing
  start. Writing a scene where the hero hesitates wastes the one thing a game cannot get back:
  the player's own willingness.
- **The hero is supposed to transform. In a game, the player does.** Skill improves, courage
  grows, and the avatar can stay flat the whole time. Stories that force the avatar's arc
  anyway often feel like the game is doing the growing for you.
- **Acts fight loops.** A game is a loop repeated hundreds of times; a journey is a one-way
  line. The middle of a journey is a low point; the middle of a game is where the player is
  best at the game. Those two curves point in opposite directions.

The result of literal copying is a catalogue of games that all start in a village, meet a
mentor, and end with a light in the sky.

## What it is actually good for

Use it as a **diagnostic**, not a template. Ask of an existing design:

- Has the player ever left a world they understood? If not, nothing has started.
- Has anything been taken away, and did the player feel it? If not, there is no ordeal.
- Does anything the player did come back changed? If not, the ending is a cutscene.

That is a pacing checklist. It catches designs that are 80% middle.

## Games that show the shape without being crushed by it

*Journey* keeps the stages and removes every word. *Hades* puts the tests inside the loop and
the return in the space between runs. *God of War* (2018) leans on it openly — and its best
moments are where the avatar's arc and the player's skill meet.

## Check yourself

??? question "Why is 'refusal of the call' the stage games usually drop?"
    Because the player has already chosen to play. A game that makes the protagonist refuse
    is refusing on the player's behalf, which reads as the game not wanting to start.

??? question "Your game is fun in the middle and dull at the end. Which stage should you check?"
    The Ordeal and the Road Back — most dull endings come from nothing being lost and nothing
    coming back changed, so the finale has no weight to spend.

??? question "Is the Hero's Journey a plot or a structure?"
    A structure. It says where pressure should fall, not who the characters are. Two very
    different games can both be on it.
