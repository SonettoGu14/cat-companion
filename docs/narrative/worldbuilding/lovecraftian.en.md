# Lovecraftian horror

In an ordinary horror story the threat is violence: something will kill you, and the question is
whether you get away. Lovecraftian horror runs on a different threat:

**Understanding is the cost.** The thing in the dark is not dangerous because it attacks. It is
dangerous because once you understand it, you are no longer the person who walked in. Knowing
too much is the wound.

That is why the weird tales of the 1920s and 1930s feel different from monster stories, and why
they translate so badly into games that only take the monster.

## A soft setting, and what that forbids

The rules are deliberately incomplete. You never learn the full map of what is out there — only
that it is larger than you and indifferent.

That has a hard consequence for design: **a soft setting cannot be used to solve problems.**
The same logic as [the worldbuilding page](../worldbuilding/index.md): the less the player
understands the rules, the more it feels like a miracle when the rules turn out to fit. In a
game, a miracle is a bug.

So the unknown in a game has to do a different job from a puzzle. It must be:

- **Recognisable but incomplete.** The player must sense a pattern — a shape, a sound, a
  recurrence — so that the unknown is a boundary, not a random number generator. "Unknowable"
  is not a licence for the designer to be lazy.
- **Inferable from evidence.** Letters, recordings, the state of the previous people who looked.
  The player assembles the picture. Being told the truth is much weaker than assembling it.
- **Never used as the solution.** If the ending is "the entity arrives and solves the plot", the
  setting has become a deus ex machina with tentacles.

## Three questions to design with

**How much does the player get to know?** Information is usually a reward. Here it should
sometimes be a price: each clue buys understanding and costs a little of the player's certainty,
or of the character's mind.

**How is the unknown shown?** Absence, scale and aftermath. The empty room in a house that is
too big. A recording that stops. The previous expedition's equipment laid out neatly.

**What does failure look like?** Not death. Death is merciful and it ends the story. The
Lovecraftian failures are: you become part of it, you cannot be helped, or you save yourself and
lose everything you were trying to protect.

## Mechanics and what they actually do

| Mechanic | What it does | How it is usually misused |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sanity or stress meter | Makes knowledge a visible resource: contact costs, understanding costs more | Becomes a second health bar that regenerates |
| Documents and recordings | Lets the player assemble the truth themselves | Becomes a collectible with a percentage counter |
| Enemies that cannot be fought | Removes the player's default solution, forces flight or subtlety | Becomes a chase sequence with a checkpoint |
| A world that reacts to being looked at | Turns observation into a risk | Becomes a jump-scare |

The sanity meter is the interesting one, because it is where the theme becomes a rule. In *Call
of Cthulhu* (the tabletop game) it is a slow erosion; in *Bloodborne* the insight stat literally
changes what you can see — gaining understanding changes the world you are walking through.
That is the strongest version: knowledge is not a number you lose, it is a door that opens.

## Three ways it fails

**Tentacles as a skin.** The aesthetic without the epistemology. If the player learns nothing
about the world's nature, the tentacles are decoration.

**"Unknowable" as an excuse.** The ending contradicts everything that came before, and the
designer calls it cosmic. That is not mystery; it is a plot hole with a philosophy attached.

**The player learns nothing.** Cosmic horror still has a shape: the shape is that understanding
is bad. If the story ends with the player unchanged, nothing was horror — it was just strange.

## Examples worth studying

- **Bloodborne** — insight changes what you can perceive, so seeing more is both progress and
  corruption.
- **SOMA** — consciousness and continuity, played as dread rather than as a puzzle.
- **Call of Cthulhu** (tabletop) — sanity as a mechanical cost of investigating.
- **Darkest Dungeon** — stress as a resource the player manages while descending.

## Check yourself

??? question "1. In your story, what does the player lose by understanding something?"
    If the answer is "nothing", you have gothic atmosphere, not cosmic horror. The loss can be
    mechanical (a stat), narrative (a relationship), or perceptual (the world looks different
    now), but it has to exist.

??? question "2. Is your unknown a boundary or a random number generator?"
    A boundary has rules the player can half-see: it always comes from the water, it never
    crosses iron, it answers in seven syllables. A random generator has no rules and therefore
    nothing to fear.

??? question "3. Could the ending be replaced by a monster fight without anyone noticing?"
    If yes, the unknown was never doing structural work. Cosmic horror ends with recognition, not
    with a boss.
