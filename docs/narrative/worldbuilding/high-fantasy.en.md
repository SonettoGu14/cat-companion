# High fantasy

High fantasy is not "a world with a lot of magic in it". It is a world where magic got into the
institutions: academies, guilds, armies, taxes, the state religion. The test is not how
spectacular the spells are — it is whether the world is organised as if magic had always
existed, the way our world is organised around electricity.

That is a much harder bar than it sounds, and most student projects miss it in the same way.

## The question that decides the whole society

**Who is allowed to use magic?**

There are only a few honest answers, and each one produces a different world. Pick one and
follow it — do not pick two.

| Who can use it | What the society becomes | The conflict it hands you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood — you are born with it | Aristocracy. Magic is the justification for why some families rule | Inheritance, legitimacy, a noble child who has none |
| Training — anyone can learn it | An education system, with tuition, exams and a class ladder | Who can afford the years, who fails out, who is self-taught |
| Contract — a god, a spirit, a patron | Religious or feudal authority. Power is borrowed and revocable | Debt, obedience, a patron who asks for something unbearable |
| Machine or object — anyone who owns the item | Industry. Magic becomes a commodity with supply chains | Monopoly, theft, maintenance, who can afford the good one |

Whichever you pick, the interesting stories are at the boundary: the person just outside the
rule. The student with no bloodline at an academy of nobles. The priest whose god has stopped
answering. The blacksmith who can make the item everyone needs.

## The test almost nobody passes: why is not everyone doing it?

If magic is cheap and common, the world must change everywhere, not just on the battlefield.

Ask four questions about your own setting:

1. **Plumbing and waste.** If a cantrip moves water, cities do not need aqueducts or wells.
2. **Medicine.** If healing is a spell, what does a hospital look like? Who pays?
3. **Transport.** If teleportation exists, why are there roads and ships — and who owns the
   network?
4. **War.** If one mage can level a wall, why does anyone still train infantry?

You do not have to answer all four in the game. You do have to be able to answer them, because
the moment a player asks, the setting either holds or it does not. If the honest answer is
"because the story needs medieval castles", the setting is a backdrop, and everything built on
it inherits that weakness.

The good answers are constraints: magic is rare, dangerous, legally restricted, monopolised by
a guild, or costs something the user cannot get back. Any of those keeps the world's shape
intact *and* gives you a rule players can plan around.

## Costs that keep it playable

A cost is what turns magic from scenery into a resource.

| Cost | What it produces in play |
| --- | --- |
| Slots or charges per day | Pacing. Players hoard, then spend — the classic D&D shape |
| A resource that regenerates | Sustain versus burst decisions |
| Something permanent — memory, years, a body part | The heaviest choices, and the ones players talk about afterwards |
| Social cost — being seen, being known | Stealth, reputation, who you can afford to be in front of |

The D&D-style slot system is worth studying even if you never use it: it is a hard rule that
makes magic feel scarce, and scarcity is what makes the player's decision mean anything.

## Three ways it fails

**Magic as the universal answer.** Every obstacle in the plot has a spell that solves it, so
the plot has no obstacles — only delays. If a spell can solve a problem, the problem was never
about the spell.

**Medieval Europe with a spell list bolted on.** Castles, kings, candles and no magic plumbing.
The setting is a costume, and the player can feel that nothing they learn about magic changes
anything they do.

**The lore dump.** Forty pages of cosmology before the first decision. The information that
matters is the information the player can act on: what can I do, what does it cost, who will
stop me.

## Check yourself

??? question "1. Your world has magic and also swords. Give one reason that is not 'because it looks cool'."
    Any answer that names a constraint works: magic is too slow for a melee, too visible, too
    expensive, legally reserved for nobles, or it fails near iron. If you cannot name one, the
    sword is a costume.

??? question "2. In your setting, who cannot use magic, and what do they do about it?"
    The people outside the rule are where the story is. If nobody is excluded, you have not yet
    decided who is included — you have only decided that magic exists.

??? question "3. Name the mechanic where the player pays a price for magic. Is it the same price the setting describes?"
    If the setting says magic costs a memory and the game charges mana, the player will believe
    the mana and ignore the codex. The mechanic is the setting that the player actually receives.
