# Worldbuilding

A setting in a game is not scenery. It is a system of rules that decides what a character
can do, what it costs, and who has the power to stop them.

The cleanest example in fantasy is the Weave. In the Forgotten Realms, magic is a fabric
covering the world, every spell is a hole torn in it, and the goddess who tends it can
withdraw magic from everyone at once. *World of Warcraft* builds the same idea out of ley
lines, the Well of Eternity and schools of magic that do not all cost the same. Neither of
those is a fact about a place. Both are answers to one question — **what can magic do, and
who controls it** — and a plot is what happens when different people give different answers.

That is the whole point of this page: a setting is structure, not decoration.

most structural work, and because they are the three most often copied from the surface.

## What a setting actually does

Three jobs. If it does none of them, it is wallpaper.

| Job | What it looks like | Without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bounds what characters can do | Rules the player learns: teleport needs a line of sight, resurrection costs a life, the vault is warded | Every problem has the same answer: "use magic" |
| Supplies a cost | Mana, corruption, sacrificed memory, a bargain with something that will collect | Choices have no weight, so the plot has no stakes |
| Generates conflict | Who controls the source, who is cut off from it, what happens when it fails | The villain is evil because the script says so |

The third one is the one most settings miss. A magic system is interesting not because it
is elaborate, but because different groups want different things from it.

## Hard settings and soft settings

There is an old rule of fantasy writing, usually called Sanderson's first law: how well the
audience understands a magic system determines whether the author is allowed to use it to
solve a problem. Games push this further than novels, because the player is not watching —
the player is planning. They count cooldowns, mana, ammo and distance, and they will find
the exploit you did not intend.

| | Hard setting | Soft setting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rules | Stated, consistent, testable | Implied, mysterious |
| The player can | Predict, plan, exploit | Be surprised, feel small |
| Good for | Puzzles, combat, systems | Horror, wonder, tone |
| Fails as | A spreadsheet | A convenient miracle at the end |

Most games want a hard core and a soft edge: the thing the player does every thirty seconds
is fully specified, and the thing that gives the world its scale is not.

## Six questions for your own setting

Ask these before you write lore. If you cannot answer one, that is the page you are missing.

1. **Where does the power come from?** A source the player can point at is a place the story
   can go.
2. **Who controls it?** Whoever controls the source has the power in the story.
3. **What does using it cost, and who pays?** The user, the world, or someone else — but
   always someone.
4. **What can it never do?** The limit is what makes the rest legible.
5. **How is society organised around it?** Guilds, castes, a black market, a ban.
6. **What happens when it breaks?** A failure state the story can use.

## The test: delete the setting

If the gameplay survives the setting being removed unchanged, the setting was decoration.

Remove the Weave from your design. Do the quests still make sense? Does the combat still
play the same? If yes, the Weave was a name in the codex, not a structure. Now try it the
other way: remove the cost of magic. If nothing about how the player plays changes either,
the cost was also decoration.

A setting that is doing structural work makes some things possible and other things
impossible, and the player feels both.

## Three ways it fails

**The lore dump.** The game opens with a history lesson about a war four hundred years ago
that nothing in the gameplay refers to. Nobody remembers it; it delays the first decision.

**Rules the mechanics ignore.** The setting says magic costs a piece of your soul. The UI
shows a blue bar that refills at a bonfire. The setting is now a contradiction the player
has to ignore, and they will stop believing the rest.

**Inflation.** Every new region adds a new magic system, a new currency, a new tier. The
player stops learning because learning stopped paying. Two systems that interact deeply beat
six that sit side by side.

## Where this sits next to the other pages

A setting is the **world layer**. The other pages here are the **plot layer**: [the McGuffin](../mcguffin.md)
is the thing that pulls the plot forward, [the Hero's Journey](../hero-journey.md) is a shape
for how it travels, [high concept](../high-concept.md) is the one sentence that holds both.

They are not alternatives. A game usually needs all four, and the most common failure is a
world layer nobody designed and a plot layer copied from a film.

## Check yourself

??? question "1. Name a game whose setting is decoration. What would you delete to prove it?"
    Look for a setting where the gameplay loop is identical in every region and the setting
    only changes the colour of the sky. The proof is that removing it changes nothing about
    what the player does — only what the player reads.

??? question "2. Your setting says magic has a price. Where does the player actually pay it?"
    Not in the codex and not in a cutscene. Name the mechanic: a stat that drops, a resource
    that is spent, an option that closes permanently. If you cannot name it, the price exists
    only in the text.

??? question "3. A player asks why they cannot teleport into the vault. What is a better answer than 'the story needs it'?"
    A structural answer names the rule and its consequence: the vault is warded against
    arrival, which is why the heist needs a person inside; or teleporting needs a destination
    the caster has seen, which is why the first act is about getting a look at it. The rule
    then becomes a puzzle instead of an excuse.
