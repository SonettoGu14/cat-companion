# Cyberpunk

Cyberpunk is not neon and rain. Neon and rain are what a cyberpunk world looks like from the
outside; the structure underneath is a question about ownership:

**High tech, low life.** The technology improves enormously and the distribution of power does
not improve at all — often it gets worse. Cyberpunk's claim is that new technology is not
automatically liberating. It arrives as a new form of control, sold to you by someone who will
repossess it.

Once you hold that, the aesthetics follow from it. The neon is there because the public space
is gone and the only light left is advertising.

## Three questions the setting is actually made of

**Who owns your body?** Not "can you get implants" — *on what terms*. Your arm is leased. The
maintenance is a subscription. The upgrade was a loan, and the lender knows where you live.
The classic image is not a person with a metal arm; it is a person whose metal arm is not
theirs yet.

**What still counts as a person?** Once a mind can be copied, backed up or edited, identity
stops being obvious. A copy that believes it is the original has the original's memories and no
legal standing. This is where cyberpunk touches horror.

**Who is watching?** The public space is privatised and monitored. Being unobserved is a
privilege, and it is expensive.

## Elements and what they do structurally

| Element | What it does as structure | How it is usually misused |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cybernetics | Makes the body a commodity: ability you can buy, maintain and lose | Treated as a plain upgrade tree |
| The corporation | Provides something nobody can replace — water, medicine, identity, security | Written as "evil" while providing nothing |
| The net / hacking | Information as power: access to someone else's systems | Treated as a minigame between story beats |
| Debt and contracts | Turns every advantage into an obligation | Forgotten once the upgrade is bought |
| Neon, rain, signs | Atmosphere — a consequence of the setting, not an argument | Treated as the definition of the genre |

The last row is the trap. A project can have all the visuals and none of the structure, and the
result reads as a costume. The reverse — structure without the visuals — still works.

## The test: take away the neon

Remove the rain, the signs and the colour grading. Does your world still have a conflict?

If the answer is "the corporation is evil and you fight it", the setting is thin: that is a
generic villain with better lighting. If the answer is "the player's body is collateral for a
debt they cannot repay, and the only way out is to become something the contract does not
cover", you have a structure, and it will survive any art direction.

## Three ways it fails

**Skin deep.** Dystopian imagery, no rules. The world looks oppressive but nothing the player
does is constrained by it.

**Technology without cost.** Implants that never fail, never need maintenance, never get
repossessed. At that point the technology is magic, and the genre's whole argument disappears.

**The corporation that provides nothing.** If the villain can be defeated without anyone losing
water, medicine or a job, the corporation was never a structure — it was a boss.

## Examples worth studying

- **Deus Ex: Human Revolution** — augmentation as a class marker: the people who can afford it
  and the people who are replaced by it.
- **Cyberpunk 2077** — the body as merchandise, and the city as a market with no exit.
- **SOMA** — what a copied mind is, as a horror premise rather than a plot twist.
- **Shadowrun** — magic and cyberpunk in one world; useful proof that these settings are
  interchangeable rule sets rather than fixed genres.

## Check yourself

??? question "1. Strip the neon from your pitch in one sentence. What is left?"
    If what is left is "a hero fights an evil company", the pitch is a genre label. If what is
    left names an ownership problem and who suffers from it, you have a setting.

??? question "2. What does the player's body cost them, and what happens when they cannot pay?"
    The interesting answer is not a stat penalty. It is a mechanic: the arm shuts off mid-fight,
    the upgrade is remotely disabled, the debt collector appears at the worst moment.

??? question "3. Why can nobody simply leave the city or refuse the technology?"
    The setting needs a reason the exit is closed — surveillance, debt, the fact that unaugmented
    people cannot hold a job. A world with a working exit is a world about something else.
